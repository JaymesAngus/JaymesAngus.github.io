# Book reviews

### Ringworld by Larry Niven
I'm no expert in the social politics of the 1970s, but I suspect that Niven's attitude towards women would have been considered gross even back then. It makes this book really hard to recommend and should be taken as a reason to distrust anyone giving it five stars. Seriously.

The hard sci-fi of Ringworld is phenomenal. The ship design, the stasis fields, the shadow squares, this must be the driving force behind Ringworld's selection as a Gollancz SF Masterwork, because god knows it wasn't the plot, or the characters, or the dialogue. There are so many interesting ideas that receive only a handful of throwaway lines (including some major plot exposition (did Niven get bored with his own story before finishing it?)).

Learning that Ringworld was originally written as a standalone novel does nothing to soften my attitude towards Niven. I finished the book feeling like I'd been cheated out of another hundred or so pages and a conclusion to the story I was promised. Maybe I'd get some closure by reading his other *Known Space* novels, but I doubt it. Larry Niven's Ringworld is perhaps better experienced through the works it inspired.

### Orcadia by Mark Edmonds
Orcadia is a beautifully crafted exploration of Neolithic Orkney through the wealth of archaeological sites preserved across its islands. Inspired by the works of Orcadian poet George Mackay Brown, Mark Edmonds approaches this prehistoric society through an intimate, almost personal point of view. We are immersed in this long-gone world through rich visual metaphors and a generous degree of speculation. Unfortunately, it didn't really work for me.

The first few chapters really drew me into the windswept, peat-smoked vision that Edmonds conjures for the Neolithic, but the imagery quickly became repetitive to the point of losing its potency. I couldn't shake the impression that Edmonds had already said everything he intended to say, leaving the latter half without much magnetism. I also have to admit to a degree of irritation regarding the claims that are never backed up by citation. I'm sure the statement that "Neolithic Orcadians knew about the Bru na Boinne (an extensive burial network in Ireland)" is well-founded and widely agreed upon in historical circles (you'd certainly hope so when it's written so decisively), but in a book full of citations it's just bizarre that certain claims are presented as gospel.

The fifth chapter exemplifies what I perceived to be a conflict in the identity of this book: a vacillation between archaeological field guide and poetic exploration of the nature of these societies. Across thirty-five pages, *The Gift of Stones* discusses how different forms of rock, sourced from locations spanning Orkney's islands and beyond, were selected and worked for their specific characteristics to produce specific handheld implements. As an inventory of notable artifacts, the chapter is too padded out with sentiments that have already been articulated at length in previous sections, like the connection between geology and human activity. Then, when Edmonds has the opportunity to discuss some of the more enigmatic relics, such as the Folsetter axe head or the carved stone spheres, he opts to forgo any conjecture regarding their purpose beyond relegating them to the mystical status of "sacred items". I don't disagree that many of the suggested explanations for the stone spheres seem unlikely, but to disregard all proposals of functional use without offering any meaningful alternative seems a missed opportunity at best and an insult to their proponents at worst.

At a modest three hundred pages, Orcadia is both too lengthy for the substance it offers and not nearly as long as it should have been.
